People Analytics Is Everywhere and Still Mostly Theater

October 2026 6 min read Selfmusing by Jiv

Companies are spending more than ever on people analytics — workforce dashboards, AI-driven hiring platforms, engagement pulse surveys, attrition predictors. The infrastructure is impressive. The ambition is real. And most of it isn't working the way anyone thinks it is.

Not because the technology is bad. Because the organizational psychology underneath it — what gets measured, whether anyone validated the inferences, and who gets to act on uncomfortable findings — is the part most companies skip. The result is analytics theater: organizations performing data-drivenness without doing the work that makes data useful.

The Facade Is Already Cracking

Two recent lawsuits make this concrete.

One AI talent platform builds candidate profiles by inferring traits like "team player" or "introvert," predicts future job titles, and ranks the quality of candidates' educational backgrounds. A significant share of its clients are Fortune 500 companies, and multiple state governments run job platforms powered by it. The lawsuit's central claim: none of these inferences were validated against candidates' actual qualifications, and candidates had no way to dispute errors in their algorithmically generated profiles.

A separate HR software provider's system was rejecting applicants within minutes. The company says the tool just "matches keywords." The judge kept the case alive on a straightforward principle: if a tool ranks, scores, or screens candidates, it's part of the hiring process. You can't outsource accountability to a vendor.

These aren't cautionary tales about uniquely reckless companies. They're examples of what most organizations do — buy the platform, point to it as evidence of being data-driven, and skip the validation work that would make the data mean something. The question is why this pattern is so common. The answer has less to do with technology and more to do with three ways organizations systematically fool themselves.

Form 1: Measuring Without Validating

In industrial-organizational psychology, validity isn't a property of a tool — it's a property of the inferences you draw from the tool's scores, applied in a specific context. A hiring algorithm validated for software engineers at one company isn't automatically valid for software engineers at another. The job demands differ, the performance criteria differ, the applicant pool differs.

When a vendor sells one model across hundreds of employers, they're making what I-O psychologists call a validity generalization claim — arguing that the tool's predictive power transfers across settings. Courts haven't accepted validity generalization as a legal defense. The defensible approach is local validation layered on top of broader evidence. Almost no one does this.

Why? Because of how organizations actually adopt analytics tools. The decision isn't usually driven by psychometric evaluation. It's driven by benchmarking — if leading companies use it, it must be prudent to follow. Both lawsuits are the logical endpoint of adoption-by-benchmarking: hundreds of organizations running the same unvalidated models because the company next door does too.

This extends beyond hiring platforms. Consider what happened when candidates started using AI to complete technical assessments. One company's coding test pass rate jumped from roughly 25% to 80% after AI tools became widely available. The test was still reliable — scores were consistent — but it had stopped measuring what it was supposed to measure. The dashboard said everything was fine. Managers started reporting weaker hires. The tool had become, in psychometric terms, reliable but not valid. Nobody noticed until the downstream consequences arrived, because nobody was checking validity — only watching the numbers.

Form 2: Measuring the Wrong Things

Even when the measurement is technically sound, organizations routinely build analytics around questions the science has already answered — while ignoring harder questions the data could actually address.

Take compensation. Most people analytics platforms are built to optimize pay structures: comp benchmarking, incentive modeling, variable pay analysis. A recent study tested what happens when you put work design characteristics — autonomy, task complexity, feedback — in the same model as variable pay. Once job design was accounted for, variable pay's relationships with work outcomes turned mostly small and negative (Gagné et al., 2026). The analytics infrastructure that most organizations invest in is answering a question the research has largely settled, while ignoring the harder question — how do we design work itself? — that the platforms can't easily quantify.

Or take generational segmentation. Companies build talent strategies, employer branding campaigns, and retention programs around "what Gen Z wants." A study of over 584,000 people across 113 countries found that generational differences essentially disappeared once you controlled for age and period effects (Schröder, 2024). Generational labeling, it turns out, functions largely as a statistical artifact once age, cohort, and period effects are isolated. The consultants sell it anyway, and the dashboards dutifully track it.

This is the criterion problem that runs through all of organizational measurement: who defines what "good performance" looks like determines whether the analytics measure the right thing. If the criterion is contaminated — if it captures what's easy to count rather than what predicts real outcomes — then every model built on it produces confident-looking noise.

Form 3: Measuring Correctly and Ignoring It

The third form of theater is the quietest. An organization invests in analytics, the analytics produce a real finding, and nothing changes.

Expectancy theory explains part of why. Managers won't use analytics outputs if they don't believe the data reflects reality, don't believe acting on it will change outcomes, or don't value the outcomes it predicts. When an algorithm says "promote her, not him," and a manager's judgment says otherwise, the manager's autonomy meets the system's recommendation — and autonomy usually wins.

But the problem runs deeper than managerial resistance. Most analytics platforms track what employees report — satisfaction, engagement scores — rather than what employees actually do. A meta-analysis covering over 20,000 employees across 58 samples found that workers are reasonably good at crafting the resource side of their jobs — seeking out task variety, building social support — but consistently struggle with managing demands (Holman et al., 2024). That distinction is exactly the kind of insight analytics could surface: where are employees successfully reshaping their roles, and where are they stuck? Instead, what gets dashboarded is the satisfaction score — a summary statistic that obscures the mechanism underneath.

Similarly, when a people analytics system tracks KPI completion rates and flags someone whose numbers are dropping, it reads disengagement as a performance problem. Recent research on goal disengagement suggests the actual mechanism is often conflict between competing goals — not stalled progress on one goal, but an unresolvable tension between two (Chhabda et al., 2026). That's a fundamentally different diagnosis, and it requires a fundamentally different intervention. No standard dashboard captures inter-goal conflict. The data says "this person is falling behind." The psychology says "this person made a rational decision about which goal to abandon." The organization sees a performance issue.

What Doing It Right Actually Requires

One of my professors put the core problem in a single line: "Just because you have numbers doesn't mean you have measurement."

Most companies operate at what could be called "drive-by analytics" — descriptive dashboards showing turnover rates, headcount, time-to-fill. They bought platforms promising predictive and causal modeling, and they use them to generate pie charts. The infrastructure exists. The methods exist. The gap is organizational.

Consider a study of 1,800 physicians where structural equation modeling was used to identify what actually drove satisfaction. The answer was leadership and communication quality — not compensation, which is what management had assumed. The finding was uncomfortable because it pointed at leadership behavior, not a budget line item. And that's the real test of whether an organization is data-driven or just data-decorated: can it tolerate acting on findings that implicate its own decision-makers?

Good people analytics starts with the organizational question — what decision are we trying to make, and for whom? — not the data question — what can we measure? It requires local validation, not borrowed confidence from a vendor. It requires criterion clarity: knowing what you're actually trying to predict, and whether your measure of it reflects the real thing or just what's easy to count. And it requires organizational willingness to act on answers that might be inconvenient.

That last part isn't a data science problem. It's an I-O psychology problem. And it's the one most organizations haven't solved.
